Glider trade-offs: wing loading, descent, and launch

September 22, 2026

In EF 157 at the University of Tennessee, I help coordinate a five-person team designing a self-propelled, self-launching glider for a competitive accuracy and payload-delivery tournament. The scoreboard cares about where the glider goes and what it carries. The engineering lives in the trade-offs that get it there.

Three knobs that fight each other

Wing loading sets how the aircraft responds to lift and how forgiving the flight path feels. Higher loading can help penetration and predictability in some regimes; it also changes stall margins and landing behavior. Descent profile is the path from launch energy to target — too steep and you miss the landing window; too shallow and you bleed energy without reaching the mark. Launch mechanism decides how cleanly you convert stored energy into a repeatable initial condition. A clever wing does not save an inconsistent launch.

None of these choices is “maximize this number.” Each one moves another.

Coordination is an engineering deliverable

On a five-person freshman team, the bottleneck is rarely a single equation. It is keeping geometry, propulsion assumptions, and scoring strategy in the same conversation. My role has included helping the team stay aligned on which trade-off we are optimizing this week, so we do not polish a wing while the launch still invents a new initial condition every trial.

What this has in common with utility work

At KUB I learned that field constraints rewrite office ideas. The glider project is smaller and academic, but the pattern is familiar: state the constraints, make the trade explicit, document the decision, then test. Manufacturing and energy internships reward that same habit — especially when the “customer” is a scoring rubric, a crew, or a safety standard.

Still in progress

This project is active (Fall 2026). I will update this note with measured results as we freeze a design and fly it. Until then, the honest status is: we are learning which trade-offs matter by arguing them with numbers, not slogans.
